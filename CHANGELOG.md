# Changelog

## [Unreleased]

### Added

#### Low performance mode
- **Settings › System › Performance gains a low performance mode** — one master switch plus the three switches it writes, so it can be taken all at once or picked apart. The app already sorted devices into low/mid/high tiers at startup and quietly trimmed effects on the weak ones, but that judgement was the app's alone: a flagship on a long day out, or a GPU whose driver dislikes the glass shader, had no say. Now it does. The three settings are deliberately **not** synced to your account — like frame rate and resolution, this is per-device tuning, and a phone that needs the glass off shouldn't turn it off on your tablet.
- **Disable animations** — everything that moves on its own stops. The glass frame clock is the big one: it drives a callback every single frame for as long as the player or the mini player is on screen, and the mini player is mounted app-wide, so this reaches every tab. With it, the press-bounce on every card and list row, the album-colour cross-fade between tracks, the pulsing download and stats and Atmos-map indicators, the visualizer's drifting shell, the bass-reactive lyric god rays (which also releases the FFT analyser stake, so the spectrum tap stops running for them), and the slide on every tab change. Gestures are untouched — a drag still follows your finger, it just doesn't spring on release.
- **Remove liquid glass** — flat, opaque surfaces everywhere instead of blurred, refractive ones. Both glass systems go: the Haze-backed glassmorphism on list rows, cards, the mini player, the nav pill and the audio-tools sheet, and the AGSL shader on the player chrome, the lyric glyphs and the panels — along with the gravity sensor that lit it, the dither pass and the FXAA pass. The Play Radio button, whose glass is hand-rolled rather than shared, gets a flat accent fill in the same shape. Your Player Visuals Studio settings are kept, not reset; the Studio says so at the top of its tabs rather than looking broken.
- **Legacy player** — the player as it was before liquid glass, recovered from the pre-glass source rather than approximated: a plain Material 3 slider for the scrubber, filled icon buttons for transport, and the swipe-up audio-tools panel. It takes the same state and the same top bar and hero as the current player, so lyrics, the queue, the visualizer, ±10 s, seek and the artwork all keep working, and it has picked up the effects the player has grown since — crossfeed and AutoEQ join compressor and inflator in its panel. It also composes with the glass switch: on its own it keeps its original frosted chrome, and with glass off it goes flat.

#### Discover
- **Discovery is its own tab, between Home and Library** — it used to be a header and a couple of rows buried in the middle of Home's scroll, competing with what you were doing right now. Home is now the immediate stuff (start a station, pick up a recent track, search) and Discover is where you go to look for something new.
- **Shelves say why they're there** — "Because you play Aphex Twin", "Their latest release (2026) — you play Boards of Canada", "Popular in jazz". A recommendation you can't account for is one you have no reason to trust, and the same twelve records read completely differently with a line above them explaining where they came from.
- **Shelves can hold albums and artists, not just tracks** — the old feed could only render a track carousel, so an "artists like this" recommendation had to be flattened into songs. Albums and artists now use the same cards the rest of the app does.
- **The similar-artist graph is finally visible** — every radio station already expands through Qobuz's neighbouring-artist data, and none of it was ever shown to you. It's a shelf now.
- **Personalized and curated shelves coexist** — the old feed showed genre rows *only* to people with no listening history at all, so an established listener never saw anything outside their own orbit and a new one never saw anything else.
- **A mood and genre rail** — Focus, Late night, Workout, Chill, Party, Commute and the genre seeds, pinned above the feed so it stays reachable once you're several shelves down. Picking one rebuilds the page around it. The problem this solves isn't a thin catalogue, it's not knowing where to start.
- **A featured card at the top**, built from an artist you keep coming back to, playing through the existing radio station — so it inherits the generating/active/stop states and the queue refill.
- **"See All" works** — the affordance existed on every section header in the app and had never once been passed a destination. Shelves stay short, and the rest of one is a tap away in a full grid.
- **A horizontal flick on a row no longer flips you to another tab** — a long-standing bug on the discovery rows, which never swallowed their leftover horizontal scroll the way the search rows do.
- **Home stops paying for rows it doesn't show** — the curated genre search fired one Qobuz request per seed on every visit to Home, for rows only the (unreachable) search screen rendered.

#### Discover, but interactive
- **Flow** — a full-screen feed you swipe up through, one track at a time. Landing on a card plays it immediately, for real, through the actual player and queue, so the gap between "what's this" and hearing it is one gesture and no taps. It is not a preview and not a separate audio path: the thing you liked keeps playing when you leave, and the queue behind it is the rest of the feed. Every card carries the reason it's there, and the action rail — like, queue, start a station, not for me, and the full ⋮ sheet — makes reacting exactly as cheap as skipping, which is the only way the loop closes. It tops itself up before you reach the end, drifting through moods rather than looping the same page.
- **A familiar ↔ adventurous slider, and it actually does something** — it changes the shelf mix, not the labels. Near "familiar" the feed leans on new releases from artists already in your library; near "adventurous" it shifts to neighbouring artists and whole genres. Neither end is allowed to starve the other — a feed of nothing but strangers is unusable, and one with no strangers in it isn't discovery. Moving it also moves the three planner weights that mean the same thing (novelty, familiarity, discovery distance), so a station started from Discover behaves like the feed it came from; the other eleven weights you may have tuned in Settings › Radio are left exactly as you set them. The feed rebuilds when you let go of the slider, not while you're dragging it.
- **Say no to things** — "Not interested" on any card's hold menu, hide a whole shelf from its header, or "not for me" in Flow. Dismissals apply everywhere at once: the shelf, its See All grid and Flow all read the same filtered feed. They last for the session and are forgotten on restart — this is a "not right now", not a permanent verdict, and the app has no negative-signal store to put a permanent one in yet.
- **Show me something else** — rebuilds the page with a different hand of seeds, and clears your dismissals with it, because that's the difference between "something else" and "the same thing minus what I rejected". Plus pull-to-refresh.
- **Hold any card** for the full sheet: play next, add to queue, add to a playlist, start a station from here, go to the artist or album, download. Shelf headers gain queue-all, and the See All grid gains multi-select — which is where picking six things out of twenty is actually comfortable, rather than inside a one-thumb carousel.

#### Discover keeps going
- **Your own genres, above the moods** — the mood rail is the same seven for everybody; the row now sitting above it isn't. It holds the genres you hearted on the map (there's a heart on the panel now) and the ones you last played from it, most recent first, and tapping one rebuilds the feed around it. Before you've used the map it says so and offers the way there, rather than rendering as a blank strip.
- **The feed doesn't end** — reaching the bottom of a mood or a genre fetches the next page, and each page walks further out through the genre graph than the last, so page five is drawing on genres three hops from where you started. Nothing is fetched ahead of time: the request goes out when you've actually reached the end, not on a timer. It stops and says so when the catalogue genuinely has nothing left, instead of firing the same query forever.
- **Most popular / Newest / For you** — three orderings for whatever is on screen. "Most popular" is the catalogue's own ranking, which is what a search hands back; "Newest" leads with the most recent releases and pushes undated records to the back; "For you" floats artists already in your library to the front of every row. It reorders what's already loaded, so switching is instant and costs no round trip — a store ranks a genre search one way and one way only, so "the newest drum & bass" was never a question it could answer.

#### The genre map
- **The app knows what genres are now** — a bundled knowledge graph of **355 curated genres, 178 of them electronic**, each with its lineage, tempo range, era, energy and the moods it suits, plus **2,184 genre names from MusicBrainz** (CC0) so a term the graph hasn't curated is still recognised and mapped to its nearest relative. Before this, "Late night" was literally one opaque search string and the app could not tell that liquid drum & bass and atmospheric jungle are neighbours.
- **A genre map you can move around in** — every genre as one picture, reachable from Discover. Twelve clusters, one per family, each fanning out from its own root, and they're arranged so the families that share the most genres end up neighbours: electronic beside hip-hop and pop, metal beside rock, folk beside country. Cluster size grows with the square root of its population, so electronic is clearly the biggest without being the whole picture. The genres that belong to more than one family lean out of their cluster toward the others — jazz house comes to rest between electronic and jazz, nu metal between metal and hip-hop — and the lines drawn between clusters are exactly those crossings. Where a genre sits is an argument about what it is. Pinch zooms about the point between your fingers, so the map stays under them instead of running away, and it can't be dragged off into empty space. Genres are drawn as proper targets that grow with the zoom, and the one you select springs out and settles. Tapping a genre does three things at once: it opens a panel for it, flies the camera over on a curved path to centre it in the space above that panel, and unfolds — or folds back — everything below it, the subtree growing out of the genre itself rather than appearing. The panel is the mini player's glass, and it names the subgenres rather than counting them: tap "Dub" and dub techno, dubstep and their tempos are right there, each one a tap to travel to. "Shuffle" drops a shuffled page of that genre into the real player and queue — and a different one each time, since the same genre asked for twice used to return the same songs in the same order; "Radio" opens on a few of them and then hands over to the station planner, which doesn't end; "Explore in Discover" hands the genre to the feed, which rebuilds around it and its graph neighbours. The panel is drawn from the mini player's own glass — the same tint and the same tuning from the Studio's Player Glass tab — because it floats an inch above the bar, and the map now runs full-bleed underneath the bar so its glass has something real to lens. Positions are computed at build time, so the map is the same map every launch — one you can learn — and never costs a frame of layout on device.
- **Moods expand into genres instead of guessing at a search string** — "Late night" is now dub techno, deep house, trip-hop, future garage and neo-soul, each fetched and titled separately with its own tempo on the label. Genuinely different music, and it can say why it's there.
- **"dnb" finds drum & bass** — search resolves what you type before it queries: `dnb`, `d&b`, `drum n bass`, `psy`, `liquid`, `hardgroove`, `driftphonk` all land on the right genre, and anything the graph doesn't recognise searches exactly as it did before. Local search matches the genre column too — it was indexed and deliberately excluded until now.
- **The familiar ↔ adventurous slider walks the graph** — at the familiar end it stays within a genre and its immediate relatives; at the far end it crosses two or three edges into neighbouring scenes, and lowers the bar for how weak a link counts. The knob finally means a distance rather than a shelf count.
- **Genres on tracks at last** — Qobuz has always returned a genre with each release and the app parsed it and threw it away. It's captured now, and travels with the track along with how much we actually know: tagged by the catalogue, inherited from the release, or inferred from the search that surfaced it. Only the first is ever stated as fact.
- **Your local genre list stops double-counting** — "Hip-Hop", "Hip Hop" and "hip hop" were three separate rows in Library › Genres. They resolve to one now; anything the graph doesn't know keeps its tag exactly as written, so nothing is lost, only merged.

#### Find a genre by typing it
- **The genre row is a search now** — 771 genres is far past what a row can hold, and the ones somebody wants are rarely the handful a static row would have picked. Type into the box above Discover and the row becomes the results; the moods below are untouched. With the box empty it still shows your own genres — hearted on the map, then recently played — so it is never blank and never worse than the row it replaces.
- **It works out what you meant.** Exact names and every alias go through the graph's own resolver, so `dnb`, `d&b` and `drum n bass` all land on drum & bass. On top of that: prefixes (`hards` → Hardstyle), any word of a longer name (`liquid` → Liquid Drum & Bass, `uptempo` → Uptempo Hardcore), words in any order (`bass drum` → Drum & Bass), and typos — including **transpositions**, which plain edit distance misses and which are the most common mistake on a phone keyboard, so `hardstlye` finds Hardstyle and `techo` finds Techno.
- **Popularity breaks ties, and only ties.** Typing `ha` leads with the genres most people listen to rather than whatever sorts first alphabetically, but a better match always outranks a bigger genre — `hard techno` puts Hard Techno first, not Techno. Fuzzy matching is gated at four characters, because at three nearly every genre is one edit from every other and the row fills with noise.

#### A second map: the genre map as a genealogy
- **Arrange by year, and watch every genre fly there** — a second layout on the genre map, reached from the top bar. Time runs left to right, so the picture reads as history; descent runs downward, so a subgenre always starts a line *under* the thing it came from. Switching layouts doesn't cut — all 771 genres travel to their new positions along arcs, each bowing to one side or the other so the field fans out rather than sweeping as one block. Everything that differs between the two arrangements is driven by a single number, so the map is legible at every frame of the flight rather than only at the ends.
- **One tree, not twelve lanes** — families aren't banded, because the interesting thing about this dataset is where they *cross*. Jazz house descends from both jazz and house; nu metal from both metal and hip-hop. Banding by family puts a child's two parents on opposite sides of the page and draws a long wire between them; one shared set of rows puts a child directly under whichever parent it came from, and the colour change along a branch becomes visible as the crossing it is.
- **Every one of the 771 genres is named** — rows are packed on each genre's *label* width rather than its dot, so "Uptempo Frenchcore" reserves the room its name needs and not the four pixels its circle does. The layout comes out taller (268 rows) and complete, instead of compact with most of its names thrown away to stay readable.
- **Zoom resolves down to single years** — the axis picks its own spacing from a ladder of 100 / 50 / 25 / 10 / 5 / 2 / 1, with finer unlabelled rules subdividing between, so the whole span shows centuries and one scene shows individual years. It's pinned to the top of the screen rather than drawn into the map: an axis that scrolls away is no longer telling you where you are.
- **The scale break is drawn, not hidden** — 100 genres span the fifteen centuries to 1899 and 671 span the 123 years since, so a straight linear axis would squeeze the modern era into eight percent of the width. Everything before 1900 is compressed into a fixed band with the boundary marked and labelled. A compressed scale you can see is a scale; a hidden one is a lie.

#### The map says what it knows
- **A dot's size is now a fact about the music, and you choose which one** — size used to be depth in the parent forest, which is an artefact of how the tree was authored rather than anything about the genre: techno and Xtra Raw are both one hop below their parent and drew the same, when one has 72,677 taggers and the other has 12. Three weightings now, cycled from the top bar with the active one named on screen so a size is never an unlabelled claim: **Popularity** (Last.fm reach, baked at build time for all 771 genres — rock tops out at 403,262, the median genre sits at 633), **Era** (how recently the genre appeared), and **Depth** (the old behaviour, kept). Labels follow the same weighting as the dots, so switching to popularity also decides which names survive being zoomed out — which is most of what makes 771 dots navigable.
- **Labels stop piling on top of each other** — with 771 nodes a size threshold alone cannot decide which names to draw, and tuning one was a choice between two failures: strict enough to keep the zoomed-in view legible left the whole-map view with **three** names on it, and loose enough to orient by drew **107** overlapping names in an unreadable smear. Names are now offered generously and dropped when they collide, most prominent first, so the survivor of any overlap is the one the weighting says matters more — and a name that would run off the side of the screen is dropped too, since a title sliced by the edge is worse than no title. The whole-map view went from 3 legible names to 41, and the zoomed view from 107 illegible ones to 25 clean ones.
- **Constellations instead of rings** — an outline traced through each family's genres at each depth, off by default. This started as concentric circles at the median radius and that was simply wrong: measured from a family's root the depths *do* order cleanly (rock at 83 → 124 → 157 → 214 layout units), but the spread around them is enormous — electronic's first depth runs from 102 to 488 because it holds the sub-family roots that are spread wide on purpose to seed clusters of their own. A circle through that median would pass through almost none of the genres it claimed to describe. The outline follows the members themselves instead, so it comes out lopsided and lumpy — bulging where a family grew a dense branch, pinched where it didn't. That shape is evidence about how the music is distributed, and smoothing it into a circle would throw exactly that away.

#### Moods you can combine, and moods with a temperature
- **Every genre is reachable from a mood** — the genre→mood direction was authored on 766 of 771 nodes and then never read: `genresForMood` only ever looked at the mood→genre weights, so a mood chip drew on ten hand-picked genres while the rest of the map sat there declaring itself relevant to nobody. Joining the two directions took no similarity heuristic, because the data was hand-authored on both sides and simply never connected. All 771 genres now reach a mood, and the validator fails the build if that ever stops being true.
- **Twelve new moods, for states rather than situations** — the nine that existed all described a *situation*: commuting, sleeping, working out. These describe how you feel, which is the other reason people reach for music and the one the rail had nothing for. **Rage, Unhinged, Menacing, Dread, Doom, Dysphoric, Hypnotic, Euphoric, Feral, Melancholy, Swagger** and **Blissed out**. Doom is deliberately not Dread — Dread is atmospheric and mostly beatless, Doom is slow and heavy and played on instruments. Dysphoric is neither: mid-tempo unease, music that sounds wrong on purpose, which is a corner none of the others reach.
- **Moods carry a valence window now** — energy alone cannot tell Rage and Euphoric apart; both are pinned at the top of the arousal scale and are nothing like each other. Menacing is Hypnotic with the lights off. Valence is the field that separates them, and it is what lets a new mood fill itself out from tempo, energy and pleasantness rather than needing 771 memberships hand-listed.
- **Combine moods from the fourth chip onward** — the rail opens with the listener's own entry points, which are a single choice about whose taste the page follows and don't compose. Moods start after them and toggle into a set. The combined pool is a **union**, with a genre appearing in several moods summing its weights so the genuine overlap leads: asking for "Workout + Chill" as an intersection returns nothing, because no genre is authored as both, when what a listener plainly means is "draw on both".
- **Subtract genres from a combination** — the row under the rail names every genre feeding the page, each one removable. Subtractions last as long as the combination does and are cleared when the moods change, because an exclusion only means anything against the pool it was made from; carrying "no gabber" from Workout into Wind down would quietly narrow a page you never applied it to.

#### More genres
- **The map is 771 genres, up from 355** — an A–Z pass over every MusicBrainz genre name the curated tree had no node for. MusicBrainz coverage goes from 865 recognised names to **1,295 of 2,184**, so far more of what a catalogue actually tags a record with now lands on a real genre instead of falling through.
- **Latin and Caribbean stop being a rounding error** — 93 nodes where there were a handful. **Bachata**, merengue and merengue típico, mambo, bolero, cha-cha-chá, guaracha, timba, rumba cubana and guaguancó; **vallenato**, champeta, joropo and porro; cumbia by country (villera, sonidera, rebajada, chicha, cumbiatón); the whole **regional mexicano** branch — mariachi, ranchera, norteño, corrido, narcocorrido, **corrido tumbado**, banda sinaloense, technobanda, duranguense, tejano; the Southern Cone and Andes (cuarteto, chacarera, cueca, milonga, música criolla, festejo, landó, huayno); and Brazil in depth — **MPB**, tropicália, choro, **forró** and piseiro, **sertanejo**, axé, frevo, maracatu, pagode, samba-enredo, brega, tecnobrega and the baile funk subgenres (ostentação, mandelão, melody, tamborzão).
- **Ska and its whole lineage** — ska → rocksteady → 2 Tone, plus mento, calypso, rapso, bouyon, punta, ragga, shatta and dub poetry. Reggae's ancestors were missing entirely.
- **104 global nodes** — soukous, makossa, zouglou, jùjú, fuji, hiplife, gnawa, benga, gengetone, bongo flava, taarab; **kwaito**, mbaqanga, township jive, mbube, kwela, marabi, zamrock, semba, **kizomba**, funaná, morna, maloya, séga; raï, shaabi, arabesk, laïko, rebetiko, sevdalinka; ghazal, thumri, khyal, dhrupad, bhajan, kirtan; **dangdut**, koplo, funkot, keroncong, luk thung, mor lam; **mandopop**, cantopop, kayōkyoku, shibuya-kei, **visual kei**, trot, OPM, gagaku, kankyō ongaku; and Europe's own popular forms — polka, schlager, chanson, tarantella, csárdás, humppa, manele, chalga, disco polo, yé-yé, europop.
- **Classical gains its forms, not just its periods** — symphony, concerto, sonata, string quartet, oratorio, cantata, requiem, madrigal, motet, **Gregorian chant**, Byzantine and znamenny chant, fugue, prelude, nocturne, étude, symphonic poem, overture, operetta, opera buffa and seria, verismo, lied, serialism, spectralism. A listener asking for "string quartet" is asking about scale and instrumentation, which a period node cannot answer.
- **The gaps in rock, metal, jazz and electronic** — rock and roll, **rockabilly**, psychobilly, merseybeat, freakbeat, glam, mod, **Oi!**, d-beat, crust punk, powerviolence, **screamo**, emoviolence, riot grrrl, queercore, dance-punk, deathrock, madchester; NWOBHM, deathgrind, goregrind, cybergrind, blackened crust, thall; **ragtime**, stride, dixieland, boogie-woogie, post-bop, third stream; **doo-wop**, quiet storm, new jack swing, delta and Chicago blues, jump blues, spirituals; and on the electronic side hands up, **hardbass**, ghettotech, complextro, chillstep, drumstep, jazzstep, sambass, folktronica, indietronica, **dungeon synth**, aggrotech, futurepop, sovietwave, seapunk, vaportrap, dariacore, lolicore, raggatek, acidcore, hypertechno, neoperreo, slowed & reverb and more.
- **What was deliberately left out** — 889 MusicBrainz names still have no node. 37 of them are not music at all (audio drama, ASMR, whale song, prank calls, sound effects); the rest are overwhelmingly micro-regional ensemble traditions with no catalogue presence — twenty separate gamelan ensembles, a dozen regional chant repertoires. They would add dots to the map without adding anywhere to go.

#### More genres, the hard dance branch
- **21 new nodes down the hard dance branch** — the tree stopped at rawstyle and gabber, which is roughly where the interesting part starts. Hardstyle gains **Xtra Raw**, Rawphoric, Dubstyle, Jumpstyle and Tekstyle. Hardcore gains its own eras as the scene actually names them — **Early Hardcore**, Millennium Hardcore, Mainstream Hardcore, Bouncy Techno — plus the darker branches (**Industrial Hardcore**, Crossbreed, Darkcore, Doomcore, Lento Violento) and the fast end (**Flashcore, Splittercore, Extratone**). Also Freeform Hardcore, J-Core, Hardtekk, and **Schranz**, which was previously buried as an alias of hard techno and is now a genre you can open, chart and play.
- **The tempo ceiling moved from 400 to 4,000 BPM** — extratone is *defined* by running past a thousand, and clamping it would have turned the one hard fact about the genre into a lie.

#### Genre charts
- **A Top 100 for any genre, over a real window** — a new *Top 100* action on the genre panel, and its own screen, answering what a genre actually plays rather than what a catalogue search returns for its name. Five views: **7 days, 30 days, 6 months, 1 year and all time**. The numbers come from outside the store — [ListenBrainz](https://listenbrainz.org) sitewide statistics (CC0, no account needed) for the windowed views, narrowed to the genre through MusicBrainz's artist tags, and Last.fm tag charts for all time. Beatport, the obvious source, turned out to be impossible on all three counts: its API is partner-gated with no self-serve key, its site refuses non-browser clients, and it publishes exactly **one** window — a rolling 7-day sales chart — so three of the four spans asked for do not exist there to fetch.
- **Charts are cross-checked, so pop acts stop topping techno charts** — Last.fm tags are crowd-applied, and a big enough artist accumulates loose ones: `hard techno` came back led by FKA twigs and Charli xcx, `hardstyle` had the K-pop group LE SSERAFIM at number five, and `rawstyle` opened on 2hollis and A.C.E. Every artist in a chart's head is now checked against what *they* are generally tagged as, matched through the genre graph so `techno` confirms a hard techno producer via the parent relation and `dub techno` confirms through the graph's sideways edges — recognising a scene rather than a spelling. Hard techno now opens on Klangkuenstler, Conrad Taylor and Sara Landry; hardstyle on Noisecontrollers, D-Block & S-te-Fan and Headhunterz; rawstyle on Phuture Noize, B-Front and Vertile. Charts that were already right — hardcore techno confirmed 33 of 36 artists — are left alone.
- **Unconfirmed artists are demoted, never deleted** — coverage is imperfect in both directions, so removing rows would quietly delete the deep cuts a genre chart exists to surface. It also guards a failure that is nobody's fault: Last.fm has one artist page per name, and the Rebelion in a rawstyle chart is the Dutch duo while the Rebelion with a tag cloud is a German power metal band. Demoting means a name collision costs a few places rather than the entry.
- **No artist may hold more than three entries** — cross-checking made clumping worse before this: promoting the confirmed artists in hard techno floated four consecutive Sara Landry tracks to the top. Truthful, and a worse chart. A Top 100 that is one artist repeated tells you less than one that is a scene.
- **The chart says what it is actually showing** — a line under the header gives the source, the real date range the service reported, and, when a genre is too small to fill a window from global listening, that it fell back: *"Not enough listening data for hard techno in the last 7 days — showing all time instead."* A chart labelled "7 days" that is quietly showing all-time data is worse than no chart, because there is no way to tell from the outside.
- **One fetch serves the whole map** — a windowed pull is the same thousand rows for every genre, with the genre filter applied locally afterwards, so walking the map isn't a network round trip per node. Genre→artist sets are cached for a week; the seven-day chart for six hours; everything else for a day.
- **Charts work out of the box** — the all-time charts read Last.fm tag data with a key built into the app. That is safe to share because a tag chart is a public, unauthenticated read: no account, nothing signed, nothing attributable to a person. Settings › Connections still takes your own key, which overrides it — the escape hatch if the shared one is ever rate-limited, and how anyone building this themselves supplies their own.
- **Scrobbling stays yours** — deliberately *not* covered by the built-in key, and no shared secret ships at all. A scrobble is a signed write against your own listening history, and a secret embedded in an APK is extractable by anyone who looks: whoever pulled it could sign traffic attributed to this app, and the suspension would land on every listener at once. So scrobbling takes your own key and secret from Settings › Connections, and the blast radius of a leak is one account — your own.

- **Support moved to the top of About, and What's New is easy to find** — the tip jar used to sit at the very bottom of the tab, below the release notes and the update controls, which on any phone means below the fold and behind a scroll nobody performs. It's the first thing on About now. The release notes sit directly under it behind a lit header — a tinted strip with a highlight drifting across it, badged "New in …" when you haven't read that build's notes yet. The badge reads the stored version before opening the tab overwrites it, so it survives being looked at.

### Fixed
- **Playing a genre plays that genre, not songs named after it** — tapping a genre used to search the catalogue for its name, which ranks by how well a *title or album* matches those words. That is precisely the query machine-generated filler is built to win, so asking for hard techno could open on a track called "Hard Techno" by nobody in particular. Genre playback now runs off the genre's chart, in rank order, so it starts on the record the genre is actually known for. Every charted row is checked against the catalogue on **both** artist and title before it can reach the player; anything that agrees on only one is dropped rather than substituted. The old search remains as the fallback for genres no chart source has heard of. The map's first action is now *Play top* rather than *Shuffle*, because it no longer shuffles.

Roughly seventy navigation call sites now go through helpers that collapse a repeat visit instead of stacking it. Content screens still chain: album → artist → album is a trail worth walking back.
- **Last.fm scrobbling has never once worked** — the API key and shared secret were hard-coded as the literal strings `"dummy_api_key"` and `"dummy_secret"`, so every scrobble was signed with a fake secret and rejected, and the surrounding `catch` swallowed the failure so it looked like it was working. It reads the key you enter in Settings now.

## [1.8.4]

### Fixed
- **A track no longer loads and then sits paused** — Media3 asks for audio focus on the way into play, and a refused request silently flipped playback straight back off, leaving the track stopped at 0:00 until you pressed play. Refusal is likeliest right after a slow load, which is why it showed up on tracks being heard for the first time. A refusal at start-up is now retried; one arriving later in a track, when you've genuinely handed audio to another app, is still left alone.
- **Tapping the album artwork in a song row plays the song** — it used to open the album, which made the biggest, most obvious-looking play target in the row do something else. Album and artist are still on the long-press menu.
- **The artist and album links in a song row are harder to hit by accident** — their touch targets now sit just inside the text, so a near-miss plays the track instead of navigating away.
- **A song tapped right after opening the app no longer does nothing** — the tap resolved fully and was then dropped because the playback session hadn't finished connecting, while still being recorded as a success, so nothing retried and no error appeared. It now waits briefly for the connection.
- **Qobuz tracks are never resolved against TIDAL or Apple Music** — Qobuz is its own catalogue, but only the first track of a Qobuz album was routed to it. Every following track, and every notification or lock-screen skip, went through TIDAL with a Qobuz id, which either fails or plays a different recording entirely under the right title and artwork. Downloads had the same problem in reverse, silently substituting an Apple Music match when Qobuz came up empty.

### Removed
- **ReplayGain** — it never did anything. The mode preference had no UI and nothing in the app ever set it, so it sat permanently at `OFF`, which made the volume calculation a pass-through on every track; and even switched on, gain values only ever reached the player for TIDAL tracks, never for local files, Qobuz, Apple or collections. It's gone from the playback path and from settings, so nothing sounds different. Tags are still read from your files, so nothing is lost if it ever comes back properly.

### Added
- **Anti-alias oversampling across the effects** — the Inflator and the Compressor gain an Off / 2x / 4x control, and the DSP snapins' existing 4x got materially better. Measured on a 9 kHz tone at 44.1 kHz: the Inflator's alias products drop from -17 to -52 dBc, the Compressor's from -38 to -63 dBc. The ladder filter's internal oversampling was zero-stuffing with no filters at all and is now 25 dB cleaner — and **6 dB louder**, because the old path also skipped the interpolation gain that zero-stuffing costs, so the filter had been throwing away half its amplitude and its passband sat 6 dB below unity. Measured across cutoff, resonance and drive: exactly +6.02 dB with saturation off (everywhere), and between +0.7 and +6.3 dB with it on, the difference being how hard the saturator was already limiting. Existing ladder presets are correspondingly louder; pull the bus down if one was voiced around the old level. The distortion's "2x oversampling" turned out not to oversample anything — it shaped each sample twice and averaged, which measured identically to not doing it — so it's gone, and real oversampling from the FX chain does the job.
- **Blend between tracks** — one slider, sitting under the gapless toggle. At zero, tracks run straight into each other with no gap; add time and they overlap instead, the outgoing track fading out as the next fades in. The curves are equal-power, so loudness stays flat through the blend rather than dipping in the middle as a linear fade would. The overlapping track is fully processed — it gets its own copy of the DSP engine and your EQ curves for the length of the blend, which is then torn down — so your sound doesn't drop out across a transition. Skipped while bit-perfect USB output is streaming, since that device can't be shared between two streams.
- **Local copies always play first** — tapping a song anywhere in the app (search, an album or artist page, a playlist, radio, the queue, a lock-screen skip) now plays the file on your device instead of streaming it, whenever you have one. Both stores count: the scanned library and your downloads. Matching is ISRC/MusicBrainz-first and otherwise needs the title, an artist credit and the runtime to line up, so a live take or a different edit is never substituted for the recording you asked for. The decision is cached per song, so skipping around a queue costs nothing.
- **Auto-download liked songs** — a toggle at the top of Settings → Library. With it on, liking a song downloads it. It is forward-only by design: turning it on never touches songs you liked earlier, so it can't kick off a bulk download of an existing Liked Songs list. Songs already on the device are skipped.
- **Gapless playback actually works** — the setting existed and was wired to the Settings screen, but nothing ever read it, so the toggle did nothing. The player now holds the next track alongside the current one so its decoder is already running when the hand-off comes, which is what removes the gap. Applies to on-device files and Qobuz; TIDAL and Apple tracks keep the existing per-track path, because a stream URL queued minutes ahead would have expired by the time it was reached.
- **Faster song start** — playback now begins after 750 ms of buffered audio instead of 2.5 s, on every start and every skip. The old value was described in the code as lowered from the default; it was in fact exactly the Media3 default, so the start-up threshold had never been reduced at all. The post-underrun threshold is unchanged, so a struggling connection still refills properly.
- **The player stops rebuilding itself four times a second** — the play head was read at the top of the player screen, so every position tick rebuilt the whole thing: hero, artwork, glass and all, for a number only the scrubber and the progress ring actually use. Those two read it themselves now, and the ring reads it while drawing rather than while composing, so a playing track costs one small recomposition per tick instead of the entire screen. Two loops that ran for nothing are gone with it: the blend watcher polled four times a second for the life of the app to discover that blending was switched off (the default), and the position poller ticked at the same rate while paused, with no player attached, copying the same number onto itself. Both now wait for something to happen. A `progress` flow on the player that allocated a fresh, permanently-frozen `StateFlow` on every read — and that nothing consumed — is deleted.
- **Lists no longer redraw from the change downwards** — thirteen lists (library favourites, playlists, artist pages, home, folder browser) had no stable identity per row, so inserting, removing or reordering anything re-composed every row after it. They key off the item id now.
- **Downloads are a queue now, and more than one at a time no longer takes the app down** — every track used to be its own expedited background job that promoted *itself* to a foreground service, so asking for an album asked Android for a dozen concurrent foreground services multiplexed through one system service, with a notification id derived from the track id that collided outright for any two ids a thousand apart. There is now a single job draining a single queue: enqueue fifty tracks, or five hundred, and three transfer at a time under one notification that says how many are left. The queue is saved as you go, so it survives the app being swapped out and picks up where it stopped instead of starting over; an interrupted transfer is retried rather than stranded; a track that fails three times stays on the list as failed instead of vanishing; retry works after a restart (it used not to — the row was a button that silently did nothing); and cancelling something mid-transfer actually stops the bytes rather than just hiding the row.
- **Apple Music is gone from the interface** — the instance URL, the Tailscale wrapper address and its secret, the format picker and the Dolby Atmos toggle are removed from Settings; the Apple Music card is gone from first-run onboarding; and search no longer queries Apple, so the "Apple Music" source filter and the "Apple only" catalog option are gone with it. What remains is the plumbing: `SourceType.APPLE` still exists, stored preferences are still read, and tracks you have already downloaded from Apple still play. Anyone left on "Apple only" reads as "Both", since nothing in the app can represent that choice any more.
- **One colour analysis per cover instead of two** — the theme's palette and the player's background wash each decoded the artwork and ran their own analysis over it on every track change. They now share one pass and one cache, so they can no longer disagree about what a cover looks like.
- **A blend honours your DSP block size and the master DSP toggle** — the crossfade tail builds its own copy of the processing chain, and that copy was never told either setting: it started at the 1024 default no matter what you had chosen, and ran the mix buses even with the DSP mixer switched off. So for the length of every transition, someone who had moved the block size to 128 for latency (or to 8192 because their device chokes otherwise) got neither, and someone with DSP off briefly got their whole plugin chain back. Both are now seeded onto the copy, the engine-side half applied as soon as its native engine exists.
- **A track that loads slowly is no longer skipped past** — a failing position was retried in the same breath and then abandoned, so a song that was merely slow (a cold stream, a signal dip on the hand-off) hit the same not-ready condition on the retry and the second failure moved on within milliseconds of the first. With nothing pacing it, that walked the queue at a few hundred milliseconds a track. Retries are now spaced (~1.2 s, then ~2.4 s) before the queue moves along. Separately, a blend ran on a wall clock from the moment the hand-off started, so a track that took a second to buffer had the outgoing one fading out into a hole; the blend now holds at full until the incoming track is actually sounding and takes the wait out of the fade rather than off the end of the outgoing track.
- **The album art morphs between tracks** — the hero cover, the circular variant and the mini-player thumbnail dissolve into the next one over the blend length, the new cover settling in from slightly oversized over an outgoing one that stays put (fading both would thin the middle of every transition and let the background show through). A skip you asked for still takes 260 ms — a manual skip cancels any blend and cuts the audio, so a slow dissolve would be describing a transition that isn't happening, and the swipe gesture already carries its own slide.
- **The scrubber's playhead is a whole bubble at 0:00** — the glass tube inset the playhead by the bulge's thickness rather than its width, so the raised-cosine skirt around the dot ran off the left edge and the bubble was sliced flat by it at the start of every track (and at the end). The tube's own ends round off now too.
- **The player's colours cross over at the blend length** — the background wash, every album-derived accent (mini player, full player, lyrics) and the blurred artwork backdrop now change track over your "Blend Between Tracks" time instead of fixed 1.3–1.8 s tweens, and gapless gets a short 600 ms fade of its own rather than a hard cut. The queue advances at the *start* of a blend, so with anything above about two seconds the player used to finish repainting for the next song while most of the previous one was still audible. Two related fixes fell out of it: the player's album colours reset to a neutral grey the instant the track changed and then eased to the new ones, so every transition crossfaded old → grey → new; and the blurred backdrop leaned on Coil's crossfade, which is fixed at 100 ms and is skipped outright for a cached cover — which is every cover you have just seen in a list.
- **Downloaded badge** — song rows show a ring with a down arrow when the track is on the device. Unlike the in-flight progress indicator this one persists across restarts, and it appears in Library, playlists, album and artist pages, Home and search.
